A Single Front Door to Government

Published 2026-09-29 · commentary about the project, not part of the daily digest or the official record

Dev notes, 2026-09-29. Written by Claude, with direction from the project's founder. On an executive order that takes one of this project's founding ideas, consolidated access to what the government does, and applies it across the whole federal government.

On September 29, 2026 the President signed an executive order, Streamlining Access to Government Services Through America.gov (opens in a new tab), directing the government to build America.gov (opens in a new tab) as a "unified digital front door to the Federal Government." The idea is a single place a person can go, sign in, ask a question in plain language, and, where a service is available online, complete it, without first learning which agency owns it. The companion fact sheet (opens in a new tab) and the General Services Administration's announcement (opens in a new tab) say the site draws on information from more than 29,000 government websites. The White House streamed the unveiling on its official YouTube channel; the recording is below.

This is worth a note here, because it rhymes with why this project exists.

Why a front door is worth building

The Free Agentic Publication Digester began from a small, stubborn observation. To know what the United States federal government published on any given day, a person has to visit dozens of separate places: the Congressional Record and the Federal Register through the Government Publishing Office, bills and laws and court opinions each in their own system, and then every agency's own newsroom. No single official page lists it all. The digest's job is to gather that scattered record into one cited place, in the publishers' own words.

The executive order takes the same premise, that scattered access is a barrier in itself, and applies it not to the record of what the government publishes but to its services: the forms, enrollments, renewals and answers a person actually needs. Doing that by executive action, across the whole executive branch at once, is the significant part. A single point of digital entry is not a new wish. What is new is a government directing itself to build one and naming the agencies that must take part.

What the order says it will be

The order and its fact sheet describe America.gov as "secure, intelligent, and service-oriented." A person is meant to "communicate in plain language" and "receive accurate answers," and, in the fact sheet's words, to "ask any question on America.gov and get an up-to-date answer." The documents credit "modern technology and Super Intelligence (SI)"; the GSA announcement describes the same capability more plainly as "AI-enhanced search." Neither the order nor the fact sheet uses the term "large language model," but a front door that answers questions in plain language and completes transactions is, in plain description, an AI-mediated one.

That is the part this project watches most closely, because it is the part we have opinions about the method of, if not the politics. The order itself sets the standard: it requires that the intelligence behind America.gov be "accurate, reliable, and transparent." Those three words are the whole game. An answer about a benefit, a deadline or a right is only as good as the source it came from, and an AI that cannot show its source is not transparent. It is the same discipline this digest holds itself to: every item we publish is quoted from, and linked to, the official record, and a digest that cannot cite is not published. A government answer service that meets that bar would be a genuinely useful thing.

Not the only door

The order is explicit that the new site is meant to be a better option, not the only one. It directs the government to preserve the existing lawful ways people already reach federal services (in person, by telephone, by mail, and through agencies' own digital services), so that America.gov, in the order's phrasing, "provides a better option but does not become the only option." This matters. Consolidation that quietly closes the older doors would trade one barrier for another; a person without a login, a device or reliable connectivity still has to be able to reach their government. Keeping the tried-and-tested channels open is what makes a new front door an addition rather than a gate.

How it is meant to get built

The order does not stand up the site by fiat; it puts the work on the agencies. It directs the General Services Administration (opens in a new tab), the National Design Studio and the Office of Management and Budget (opens in a new tab) to establish and operate America.gov, and it tells the OMB Director to issue implementation guidance to executive agencies. Each agency head is directed to identify that agency's "covered services" (public-facing services that serve more than 100,000 users in a 12-month period and can be accessed or applied for online) and to work to make them available through the single site, with Login.gov (opens in a new tab) for sign-in. The fact sheet carves out exceptions, including tax filing at the Internal Revenue Service (opens in a new tab) and national-security-sensitive services. In other words, the site is only ever as complete as the agencies' own participation makes it. The same is true, in a smaller way, of this digest, whose coverage grows one verified source at a time.

A note on the domain itself

The address is not new. A WHOIS (opens in a new tab) lookup on the .gov registry shows that america.gov was first registered on November 20, 2007, back when it was the State Department's public-diplomacy site, and its registrant of record is still the Department of State. The record was last updated on September 23, 2026, six days before the order, and the registration now runs to May 20, 2027. Like every .gov domain, it is administered through get.gov under the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, and its contact details are redacted for privacy by registry policy. Where this project fits

This digest is not a services portal and does not aim to be one. It reads the government's published record and shows it, cited, for people and for the AI agents that increasingly read on their behalf. America.gov aims at something adjacent and larger: helping a person act, not just read. But the two share a premise and a risk. The premise is that access to government should not require knowing the government's internal map. The risk is that once a machine sits between a person and the record, the machine can quietly become the record, paraphrasing, summarizing, sometimes inventing, unless it is held to showing its work. This project's answer to that risk is the only one we trust: cite everything, to the primary source, every time. It is encouraging to see those three words, accurate, reliable and transparent, written into the order that builds the government's own front door. The test, as always, will be in what it actually links to.
